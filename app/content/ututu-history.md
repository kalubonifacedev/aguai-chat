# Ututu History

## Overview

Ututu is a peaceful community located in Arochukwu Local Government Area of Abia State, Nigeria. It shares a common boundary between Aro and Ihechiowa in Abia State.

The geographical area known as Ututu is named after the founder of the Clan, Mazi Otutu Ezema. The original patriarch was Atita Akpo, a notable warrior of the Oba of Benin during the 15th century.

## Origin: Atita Akpo and the Migration

Atita Akpo is said to have fled the Bini kingdom along with his followers. Oral history has it that they first tried to settle in Ogoni land, in Indoni and Andoni. After spending a considerable length of time in both places, serious war erupted between the newcomers and their Ogoni landlords, and they left Ogoni land. How long the Atita group spent in Ogoni land before proceeding to Isieke Ibeku is still the subject of ongoing research.

## Isieke Ibeku and the Sons of Ezema Atita

At Isieke Ibeku, their new place of abode, Nna Atita Akpo, the leader, had long died. It was his son Ezema Atita who assumed leadership of the group. How long the Atita group spent in Ibeku is still a matter of conjecture.

It was from Ibeku that the three sons of Ezema Atita, namely Uduma, Onyerubi and Otutu, left to establish the Clans of Ohafia, Abam and Ututu respectively. Another son of Ezema Atita, Lokpa, established the Clan of Lokpanta.

## The Challenge of Chronology

The biggest challenge in writing the history of Ututu is to give a chronology of the migration, from the time Atita Akpo fled the Bini Kingdom to the time Otutu Ezema arrived at Amaeke Ututu.

## Arrival at Amaeke Ututu

Otutu Ezema, son of Ezema Atita, arrived at Ututu with a group of people of whom he was the leader. The area was sparsely inhabited by the Ibibios, so their first task was to subdue the Ibibios and chase them out of what would eventually be the Ututu ridge.

The name Amaeke was derived from Amaeke-Ibeku, the former home of Ezema Atita, the father of Otutu Ezema. The area called Amaeke was exclusively reserved for Otutu Ezema and his group.

## Cheke Ukwu and Nzerem (Abuma Ututu)

A certain hunter and his son strayed into the area. Their manner impressed the warriors, so instead of dealing with them as they did the Ibibios, the man's courage and intelligence endeared him to the warriors and he was left unharmed. This was unusual in those days.

The hunter's name was Cheke Ukwu, and his son was called Nzerem. Recognizing the security advantage of this powerful group of warriors, Cheke Ukwu and his son pleaded to stay under the protection of Otutu Ezema and his group. The request was granted on the condition that they would not reside in Amaeke. They eventually came to be known and called Abuma Ututu.

## The Hearthstone (Nkuma Etiti-Ogo)

The first significant action Otutu Ezema took after deciding to reside in the area was to plant his Hearth Stone (Nkuma Etiti-Ogo) as the centre of his domain. When other villages later sprang up, each new village followed his example and established its own hearthstone. The hearthstone of Amaeke Village remains the common property of the entire Ututu Clan.

## Security Arrangements and the Akpa

The initial period of settlement was turbulent, as the Ibibios continued to make incursions. Serious security arrangements had to be put in place as a deterrent. After sparing the lives of Cheke Ukwu and his son Nzerem, they were commissioned to reside in a strategic area within the Ututu ridge to act as spies in case of attacks by the Ibibios.

At this time there was another group, originally of the Ekoi tribe, who made their living as mercenaries touring the Igbo and Ibibio hinterlands in search of clients. They were known as the AKPA. Initially they were patronized by Otutu Ezema on a temporary basis, but the association became so successful in checking intruders into the Ututu ridge that Otutu Ezema decided to make the relationship permanent.

## The 1932 Account of Chief Kalu Ifere

In the intelligence report on the origin of the Ututu people, compiled by Mr. R. Newton in 1932, Chief Kalu Ifere of Obiagwulu Ututu (an Akpa village) said concerning their abode:

"When Okennachi lived in Utughugwu Aro. Ezenama lived in Ututu. Both of them had trouble with the Ibibio. When we (Akpa) came to Ututu the first man we met was Ezenama. The people of Abuma were also hired as soldiers but the Ibibio chased them back to Omuma in Ohafia. Okennachi and Ezenama combined. They met at Ugwuakuma and divided into two parts in case the Ibibio should come back. They tied palm leaves on themselves so that they should be recognized. Ezenama gave white cloth, a ram and a white fowl to 16 Akpa as a sign that they should live near him and that he accepted them. We lived first at Abuma but later on Ezenama wanted us to live nearer to Amaeke."

Note on this account: while the Chief's account might not entirely be the truth, there is no historical evidence that Otutu Ezema (called Ezenama in the account) lived at the same period as Okennachi, one of the founders of Arochukwu. There is also no evidence that Abuma (Cheke Ukwu and his son Nzerem) were chased to Ohafia as the Chief narrated. However, the account gives insight into the manner in which Otutu Ezema admitted the Akpa into the Ututu Confederacy.

## The Ututu Confederacy

"Confederacy" is a convenient term to describe the first set of people known as Ututu people. The name Ututu is derived from Otutu Ezema, leader of the group that first occupied Amaeke-Ututu, where his hearthstone was placed. The confederacy consisted of four groups:

1. **Otutu Ezema and his group**, at Amaeke Ututu.
2. **Cheke Ukwu and his son Nzerem**, who eventually became known as Abuma Ututu.
3. **The AKPA mercenaries**, who would give birth to a number of other villages in Ututu.
4. **Ohomja**, an obscure village whose people had independently come to the Ututu ridge from the Itumbuzor area, but who exploited the security advantage by aligning with the Otutu Ezema group and thereafter became part of the confederacy.

## The Offor Ututu

The instrument these four groups had in common was the Offor, which Otutu Ezema inherited from his father Ezema Atita. It became known as Offor Otutu in his new settlement. The Offor was largely responsible for keeping intruders out of the Ututu ridge. Like the "Ark of Covenant" carried by the Israelites to battle zones, the Offor Ututu accompanied the Ututu warriors in all major skirmishes with the Ibibios and gave them victory.

## The Family of Otutu Ezema

A few decades after Otutu Ezema and his group arrived, they were consolidated in their new home and began the task of governance to ensure law and order.

Oral history tells us that Otutu Ezema had many wives and children, but two sons deserve special mention: his first son **Akuma Ukwu** and his second son **Koko Ukwu**.

### Akuma Ukwu

Akuma Ukwu lived with his father at Amaeke Ututu until he was old enough to establish his own home. The residence of Mazi Otutu Ezema is known as **Ihu-Izu**, the most sacred spot in Ututu. Akuma Ukwu went to another part of the village and founded his own compound, which is called to this day the **Ndi Eziakuma Compound**.

### Koko Ukwu

Koko Ukwu did not reach the Ututu ridge with his father. He settled northward in an area called **Ujara**. Oral history tells us that at this period the Ihechiowas did not reside in their present abode. Isii Koko, Koko Ukwu's son, also lived in Ujara throughout his lifetime. To ensure peaceful existence in this area, Koko Ukwu established the deity of **Anyunku**.

## Dating the Migration

The Intelligence Report by the Assistant District Officer, Aro, said: "A good deal of information was obtained by means of stories, legends, family histories and pedigrees, and the history of Ututu can be written with a good chance of accuracy even in details, the general outline is certainly correct."

Mr. Mathews, the Government anthropologist, stated in his report that the founding of the Aro Confederacy took place between 150 and 160 years ago.

The Ututu, however, say that they arrived in their present country before the AKPA came from over the Cross River. They had trouble with the scattered bands of Ibibio living on what is now the Ututu ridge, and a party of their soldiers was driven back northward by the Ibibio. When the AKPA came, the Ututu took advantage of the war to drive out the Ibibio.

Mr. Mathews's method of finding the rough date of the AKPA invasion, by counting generations from the leaders of the invasion, was followed in the present case. The most detailed and carefully compiled pedigrees go back some nine, ten or eleven generations. Every effort was made to make certain that collateral relations were not included in the time of descent.

The report concluded that the Ututu migration took place between 180 and 200 years ago (as counted at the time of that report). It was certainly gradual, which may account for discrepancies in pedigrees. The care with which the pedigrees were kept illustrates the statement of Mr. D. A. F. Shute, District Officer, Aro, who wrote that "descent from an ancestor who brought his people into new lands is more likely to be remembered than descent from an ancestor who just lived and died where he was born."

## Akuma Ukwu, the Offor Ututu and AKPA ASAA UTUTU

When Otutu Ezema died, his son Akuma Ukwu inherited the Offor Ututu, the instrument with which his father had kept together the several groups that now inhabited the Ututu ridge. It had become the symbol of Power, Truth and Rulership.

Akuma Ukwu established a permanent abode for the Offor Ututu in his compound, Ndi Eziakuma. This abode is called **Awada Ukwu Ututu**, or the Ututu Supreme Court / House of Assembly. Whereas Otutu Ezema had the dream of making a home for his family and group in this area, it was Akuma Ukwu who made the dream a reality by establishing the body known as **AKPA ASAA UTUTU**.

Akuma Ukwu, referred to as Eze Ututu, was the one who established the seven stones from which members of AKPA ASAA derive their power.

## Notes for the Storyteller (unresolved points)

- The exact chronology of the migration, from Atita Akpo fleeing Benin to Otutu Ezema arriving at Amaeke Ututu, is not settled. Do not state exact dates or years spent at each stop.
- The time spent in Ogoni land and at Isieke Ibeku is not known.
- The 15th-century date refers to Atita Akpo's time in the service of the Oba of Benin. The 180 to 200 year estimate in the colonial-era report refers to the Ututu migration itself.
- Chief Kalu Ifere's 1932 account is treated as useful but not entirely reliable.
- This history is a work in progress. Topics not covered here (later villages, present-day leadership, festivals, and so on) are not yet recorded.
